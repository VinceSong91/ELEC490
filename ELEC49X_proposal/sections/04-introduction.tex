\CourseSubsection{The shortfalls of autonomous golf carts}
Walking golfers can already buy electric push carts that follow or lead the way on the course \cite{stewartQFollow}. So, the problem of carrying your own clubs has already been solved, but where these carts fall short is the ability to give the golfer any information about their round. The carts can't tell you the distance to the green or notice a club left behind, which is where the purpose of the \ac{ASCC} comes in. Most golfers either track their information on paper, get info from their watch, phone, or a range finder \cite{arccosSmartSensors,arccosAppleWatch,hall2025golfGps}. The \ac{ASCC} is an all in one solution to club carrying, club tracking, and distance to the green. A golfer would usually need to manually input, or accept their distance, club information on their phone or watch. The ideal user of this cart is a golfer who wants to keep track of their clubs and distances, stay off their phone, and not have to keep track of a separate tracking tool from their cart.

\CourseSubsection{A cost effective, phone-free autonomous smart caddy}\label{sec:project-objectives}
The objective of this project is to design and prototype a simple tracking golf caddy that follows the golfer safely, keeps an inventory of the clubs in the bag, and shows the distance to the green on an outward-facing display, all without any input from the golfer. Each outcome is measurable. The cart must hold its following distance (PF-03), respond to obstacles (PF-05), and detect tagged clubs (IF-04) within the targets in Table~\ref{tab:proposal-specs}, and its missing-club warnings and distance readings are checked by the tests in Section~\ref{sec:testing}. Shot-distance logging and personalized club recommendation would build on the same sensors, but they are extensions and not committed outcomes. The engineering involved is motor control, embedded sensing, golfer tracking, \ac{GPS} processing, and interface integration. Person-following robots track their target with cameras, laser rangefinders, or radio ranging, and the choice trades cost against robustness to lighting, occlusion, and bystanders \cite{islam2019personFollowing}. Commercial following carts avoid the perception problem by tracking a handset that the golfer carries \cite{stewartQFollow}. For golf distances, Hall et al. tested two club-tag \ac{GPS} systems against laser and tape measurements and found typical distance errors of three yards or less, but one system missed 67 shots where the other missed 3 \cite{hall2025golfGps}. Consumer \ac{GPS} is therefore accurate enough for a distance reading, while detecting each shot automatically is the harder problem, which is why shot logging is left as an extension.

The \ac{ASCC} differs from these systems in where it senses. A reader in the bag detects each club leaving and returning, instead of a sensor on each club detecting the swing, so the cart knows what is in the bag and can warn about a club left behind. Distances are shown on the cart instead of on a watch or phone. The cost is that the cart measures from its own position, not the golfer's, so the reading is offset by however far the cart stands from the ball. \CourseSubsection{Deliverables: follow-me mobility, club inventory, and distance display}
The committed deliverable is a single working prototype cart, demonstrated outdoors on open, dry grass with one golfer and one bag of tagged clubs. Its inputs are the golfer's position, club tag readings, \ac{GPS} fixes, obstacle detections, and a remote stop button. Its outputs are wheel motion and a display showing the club inventory, missing-club warnings, and the distance to the green. The three subsystems share the cart's position: it sets how the cart follows, it gives the distance to the green, and it tells the cart that it has moved on while a club is still out of the bag. Tagging clubs is a strategy also used by commercial shot trackers \cite{arccosSmartSensors}.
