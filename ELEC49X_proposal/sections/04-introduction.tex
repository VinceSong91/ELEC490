\CourseSubsection{The shortfalls of autonomous golf carts}
Walking golfers can already buy electric push carts that follow or lead the way on the course. So, the problem of carrying your own clubs has already been solved, but where these carts fall short is the ability to actually guide how a golfer plays the game. The carts can't act like a human caddy would, which is where the purpose of the Autonomous Smart Caddy Cart comes in. Most golfers either track their information on paper, get info from their watch, phone, or a range finder. The ASCC is an all in one solution to both club carrying, and caddy assistance. A golfer would usually need to manually input, or accept their distance, club information on their phone or watch. The ideal user of this cart is a golfer who wants to track their information, stay off their phone, and have a cheaper solution than the cost of a human caddy and electric cart combined. \CourseSubsection{A cost effective, phone-free autonomous smart caddy}\label{sec:project-objectives}
The objective of this project is to design and prototype a simple tracking golf caddy that follows the golfer safely, identifies club usage without any input, and gives personalized advice to the golfer through a display and or speaker. 
The project succeeds if the cart:
follows one golfer at a set distance and stops for obstacles;
detects each club's removal and return, and warns when a club is left behind;
estimates shot distances from GPS positions at successive club removals;
recommends a club from the golfer's own recorded distances; and
does all of this through an onboard display, buttons, and audio.

\CourseSubsection{Deliverables: follow-me mobility, smart club tracking, and onboard caddy assistance}
The deliverable is a single working prototype cart, demonstrated outdoors on open, dry grass with one golfer and one bag of tagged clubs. Its inputs are the golfer's position, button and voice commands, club tag readings, GPS fixes, and obstacle detections. Its outputs are wheel motion, displayed and spoken information, and warnings. It has three major components:

Autonomous mobility moves the cart: motors, golfer tracking, obstacle detection, and a manual override that always takes priority.
Smart bag sensing identifies each club and reports when it leaves or returns to the bag, a tagging strategy also used in [cite RFID inventory or club-tracking source].
Golf intelligence and interface stamps each club event with a GPS position, estimates shot distances, and answers the golfer's queries.
The components meet at the club event: a removal triggers a position record, and the distance to the next removal becomes the previous shot's estimated length.
The project boundary excludes full course navigation and travel between holes without the golfer. It assumes one golfer in clear view, a usable GPS signal, and green locations loaded in advance for the test site. The approach has several limitations:

Weather: the prototype will not be waterproof and is intended for dry conditions only.
Terrain: it will not handle steep or uneven ground as well as a commercial cart costing around 3000 [cite], and it is limited to fairway and light rough.
Position accuracy: consumer GPS is accurate only to a few metres [cite], so short shots and putts cannot be measured reliably.
Shot location: the cart records its own position, not the ball's, so estimates degrade when it is parked away from the shot.
Endurance: battery capacity is sized for a demonstration, not a full 18-hole round.

The primary deliverable for this project is a fully integrated, low-power smart golf caddy prototype capable of operating across standard 18-hole terrain under typical outdoor environmental conditions.
\subsubsection*{System Boundary and Operating Context}
The system operates within the physical boundary of standard outdoor golf course terrain (grass, fairways, mild incline pathways up to $15^\circ$). Inputs to the system include real-time distance/angle data from user tracking beacons, wheel encoder feedback, and manual override controls via a handlebar/remote toggle. Outputs consist of differential motor drive power signals, audio/visual alerts for unreturned clubs, and course/distance readouts displayed on an onboard user interface.

\subsubsection*{Major System Components}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Main Controller Unit (MCU/SBC):} Coordinates sensor telemetry, executes path-following logic, and monitors battery state of charge.
\item \textbf{Sensor Array (UWB + Ultrasonic/LiDAR):} Measures precise range and bearing to the user while detecting ground obstacles and steep drop-offs.
\item \textbf{Club Inventory Subsystem:} Uses short-range RFID tags on club grips to audit active equipment status in real time.
\item \textbf{Motor Controller and Drive System:} Dual-channel MOSFET motor driver with closed-loop PID velocity control to ensure smooth acceleration on variable grass textures.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection*{Limitations and Assumptions}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Terrain Constraints:} The prototype assumes operation on standard course turf and pathways; extreme mud, deep sand traps, and water hazards are outside the current operational scope.
\item \textbf{Environmental Conditions:} Optical/camera tracking accuracy may degrade in heavy rain or dense fog, requiring the system to fall back on low-frequency RF rangefinding.
\item \textbf{Payload Limit:} The chassis is rated to transport standard bag weights up to $15\text{ kg}$, excluding passenger ride-along capabilities.
\end{itemize}
