\CourseSubsection{The shortfalls of autonomous golf carts}
Walking golfers can already buy electric push carts that follow or lead the way on the course \cite{stewartQFollow}. So, the problem of carrying your own clubs has already been solved, but where these carts fall short is the ability to actually guide how a golfer plays the game. The carts can't act like a human caddy would, which is where the purpose of the \ac{ASCC} comes in. Most golfers either track their information on paper, get info from their watch, phone, or a range finder \cite{arccosSmartSensors,arccosAppleWatch,hall2025golfGps}. The \ac{ASCC} is an all in one solution to both club carrying, and caddy assistance. A golfer would usually need to manually input, or accept their distance, club information on their phone or watch. The ideal user of this cart is a golfer who wants to track their information, stay off their phone, and have a cheaper solution than the cost of a human caddy and electric cart combined.

\CourseSubsection{A cost effective, phone-free autonomous smart caddy}\label{sec:project-objectives}
The objective of this project is to design and prototype a simple tracking golf caddy that follows the golfer safely, identifies club usage without any input, and gives personalized advice to the golfer through a display and or speaker. Each outcome is measurable. The cart must hold its following distance (PF-03), respond to obstacles (PF-05), and detect tagged clubs (IF-04) within the targets in Table~\ref{tab:proposal-specs}, and its shot-distance estimates and missing-club warnings are checked by the tests in Section~\ref{sec:testing}. The engineering involved is motor control, embedded sensing, golfer tracking, \ac{GPS} processing, and interface integration. Figure~\ref{fig:intro-system-context} shows the system in its operating context.

Person-following robots track their target with cameras, laser rangefinders, or radio ranging, and the choice trades cost against robustness to lighting, occlusion, and bystanders \cite{islam2019personFollowing}. Commercial following carts avoid the perception problem by tracking a handset that the golfer carries \cite{stewartQFollow}. For shot tracking, Hall et al. tested two club-tag \ac{GPS} systems against laser and tape measurements and found typical distance errors of three yards or less, but one system missed 67 shots where the other missed 3 \cite{hall2025golfGps}. Detecting the shot event is therefore the harder problem, not measuring its length.

The \ac{ASCC} differs from these systems in where it senses. It detects a club leaving the bag instead of the swing, and it measures from the cart's position instead of the golfer's. This removes the phone and watch, at the cost of two errors that the test plan must quantify: a club removal that is not a shot, and the offset between the cart and the ball. \CourseSubsection{Deliverables: follow-me mobility, smart club tracking, and onboard caddy assistance}
The deliverable is a single working prototype cart, demonstrated outdoors on open, dry grass with one golfer and one bag of tagged clubs. Its inputs are the golfer's position, button commands, club tag readings, \ac{GPS} fixes, and obstacle detections; its outputs are wheel motion, displayed information, and warnings. The subsystems meet at the club event: a removal triggers a position record, and the distance to the next removal becomes the previous shot's estimated length. Tagging clubs in this way is a strategy also used by commercial shot trackers \cite{arccosSmartSensors}.
