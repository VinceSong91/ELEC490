\CourseSubsection{The shortfalls of autonomous golf carts}
Walking golfers can already buy electric push carts that follow or lead the way on the course \cite{stewartQFollow}, so the problem of carrying your own clubs has been solved. Where these carts fall short is that they can't tell you the distance to the green or notice a club left behind. Golfers get that information from a watch, phone, or range finder instead \cite{arccosSmartSensors,arccosAppleWatch,hall2025golfGps}, usually with some manual input. The \ac{ASCC} is an all in one solution for the golfer who wants to keep track of their clubs and distances, stay off their phone, and not carry a separate tracking tool.

\CourseSubsection{A cost effective, phone-free autonomous smart caddy}\label{sec:project-objectives}
The objective is to design and prototype a golf caddy that follows the golfer safely, keeps an inventory of the clubs in the bag, and shows the distance to the green on an outward-facing display, all without input from the golfer (Figure~\ref{fig:intro-system-context}). Each outcome is measured against Table~\ref{tab:proposal-specs} and tested as described in Section~\ref{sec:testing}: following distance (PF-03), obstacle stop (PF-01), remote stop (PF-05), club tracking (IF-03), and distance to the green (IF-02). The engineering involved is motor control, embedded sensing, golfer tracking, \ac{GPS} processing, and interface integration.

Person-following robots track their target with cameras, laser rangefinders, or radio ranging \cite{islam2019personFollowing}, and commercial following carts track a handset that the golfer carries \cite{stewartQFollow}. Hall et al. found that club-tag \ac{GPS} trackers measure distances to within about three yards but can miss shots \cite{hall2025golfGps}, so automatic shot logging is left as an extension. The \ac{ASCC} instead detects clubs leaving the bag and shows distances on the cart. The trade-off is that it measures from the cart's position, not the golfer's. \CourseSubsection{Deliverables: follow-me mobility, club inventory, and distance display}
The deliverable is one working prototype cart, demonstrated on open, dry grass with one golfer and one bag of tagged clubs, a tagging strategy also used by commercial shot trackers \cite{arccosSmartSensors}. Its inputs are the golfer's position, club tag readings, \ac{GPS} fixes, obstacle detections, and a remote stop button. Its outputs are wheel motion and a display showing the club inventory, missing-club warnings, and the distance to the green. The main limitations are dry-weather operation, restricted terrain, and distances measured from the cart, not the ball. Table~\ref{tab:intro-deliverables} summarizes the deliverable, and Table~\ref{tab:intro-deliverables-full} of Appendix~\ref{app:intro-tables} lists the assumptions, limitations, and uncommitted extensions.
