\CourseSubsection{Motivation and Problem}

Golfers who prefer walking the course must transport their clubs and equipment between shots, either by carrying a golf bag or manually operating a push cart. While conventional electric push carts reduce the physical effort required to move equipment, many still require direct user control. Commercial autonomous carts provide golfer-following capabilities, but these systems primarily address equipment transportation rather than the broader challenges of managing golf clubs and accessing useful information during a round.

An additional challenge is ensuring that all clubs are accounted for throughout play. Golfers frequently remove clubs from their bags at different locations, creating opportunities for equipment to be misplaced or left behind. Existing solutions for autonomous transportation and golf equipment management are often implemented separately, requiring golfers to rely on multiple devices or manual checks.

The Autonomous Smart Caddy Cart (ASCC) aims to address these limitations by combining autonomous equipment transportation with club identification and tracking in a single system. The project focuses on developing a practical prototype that reduces the need for manual cart operation while helping golfers manage their equipment.

\CourseSubsection{Project Objectives}

The primary objective of the ASCC is to develop a motorized golf push cart capable of following a golfer while maintaining safe operation in a controlled outdoor environment. The system will use a controller-based following mechanism rather than computer vision, reducing the computational complexity associated with visual target detection and tracking.

The cart will incorporate distance sensors to detect obstacles and support automatic stopping or avoidance. An independent emergency-stop mechanism will provide an additional means of preventing unintended motion. In parallel, an RFID-based club-tracking subsystem will identify tagged golf clubs and determine whether expected clubs are present in the bag.

The completed prototype is intended to demonstrate the integration of these functions into a single platform, with emphasis on reliable subsystem operation, safe movement, and clear communication of equipment status to the user.

\CourseSubsection{Project Scope}

The project will prioritize motorized movement, golfer following, obstacle detection, emergency stopping, and RFID-based club identification. These functions form the minimum viable system and will guide the design requirements, subsystem prototypes, and verification and validation procedures.

Additional functionality, such as collecting shot data, recommending clubs, or providing a mobile application, may be considered if time and resources permit. These features are excluded from the core project scope because they introduce additional sensing, software, and interface requirements that are not essential to demonstrating the primary system objectives.

The ASCC is intended as a prototype rather than a commercially deployable product. Testing will focus on controlled indoor environments followed by representative outdoor conditions. Operation on public roads, highly congested areas, and extreme terrain is outside the intended scope.

\CourseSubsection{System Overview and Operating Scenario}

The ASCC consists of a motorized push-cart platform, an onboard computing system, motor-control electronics, distance sensors, a golfer-following interface, and an RFID-based club-tracking subsystem. The Raspberry Pi will coordinate high-level system functions, while the motor-control electronics will regulate movement and respond to safety-related inputs.

During normal operation, the golfer activates the system and begins walking along the course. The cart receives information from the following mechanism and adjusts its movement to maintain an appropriate distance from the golfer. Distance sensors monitor the surrounding area for obstacles, allowing the control system to stop or modify movement when an obstruction is detected. The golfer can also activate an emergency stop when immediate intervention is required.

When clubs are returned to or removed from the bag, the RFID subsystem attempts to identify their presence. The system maintains a record of detected clubs and can notify the golfer when an expected club is missing.

The system boundary includes the cart, its onboard electronics, sensors, power supply, and club-tracking hardware. External elements include the golfer, the following controller, tagged clubs, obstacles, and the operating terrain. The design assumes a suitable walking surface, adequate battery charge, and environmental conditions within the operating limits of the selected hardware.

\CourseSubsection{Design Constraints}

The ASCC must be developed within the project budget of CAD \$600 and the available capstone development schedule. The design therefore emphasizes reuse of existing components, including the Raspberry Pi and ultrasonic sensors, together with a second-hand motorized push-cart platform.

Hardware compatibility, sensor reliability, battery capacity, and safe motor operation will influence the final design. Particular attention will be given to detecting obstacles, preventing unintended movement, and ensuring that the cart can be stopped reliably.

These constraints establish the basis for the functional decomposition, requirements specifications, development approach, and verification and validation procedures presented in the following sections.
